After more than two decades in international trade and investment promotion, the same handful of failures account for almost every failed market entry we are asked to rescue. None of them is a failure of strategy. Firms arrive in Africa with a sound thesis, a funded plan and a good product, and lose money for structural reasons that were decided in the first sixty days.

This article sets out where that goes wrong, in the order it usually goes wrong.

## Getting the sequence wrong

The most expensive sequencing error is choosing a partner before establishing the regulatory position.

A foreign company that has not settled whether it may hold 100% of the equity, whether its sector permits foreign ownership at all, what authorisation it needs and what its repatriation position looks like, will negotiate a partnership agreement against an unknown set of constraints. That agreement then has to be renegotiated once the real position is established — usually with less leverage and a visible deadline.

The regulatory file is not the glamorous part of market entry. It is also the part that determines what every commercial option is actually worth. Establish it first, even if that means spending a month before committing to anyone.

## Choosing the wrong entry mode

Five modes cover almost everything:

- **Direct export** — lowest commitment, highest working-capital and compliance burden per unit. Appropriate when demand is proven and the product is easily shipped, and generally a poor fit for regulated or bulky goods.
- **Distributor or agent** — fast time to market, but you are handing over margin and control together.
- **Joint venture** — shares capital and risk with a party who knows the market. Requires genuine alignment, which is rarer than it sounds.
- **Acquisition** — control from day one, at a price that reflects whatever the seller knows that you do not.
- **Wholly-owned subsidiary** — full control and full cost. Sensible once you have volume to amortise it.

The mistake is defaulting to a distributor because it is the fastest option, then treating eighteen months of poor performance as evidence the market is wrong rather than evidence the channel is wrong.

## Treating a distributor as a strategy

A distributor agreement is a channel decision. Firms routinely promote it to a market strategy, and then discover they have neither built demand nor created a proprietary relationship. The counterparty's existing network is the entire asset, and you do not own it.

Three questions separate a workable distributor relationship from a comfortable one:

1. Are they selling anything else in your category? If yes, you are a line item, and you will be deprioritised the moment a competitor pays better.
2. What specifically will they do that a competitor's distributor will not? If the answer is "introduce you to buyers", you are buying introductions, which do not compound.
3. What happens to the customer relationship at the end of the agreement? If every buyer belongs to them, you have built a business you will have to buy back.

If you want a real channel, you need exclusivity, a defined territory, agreed marketing obligations on both sides, and a review mechanism with teeth. Exclusivity is the one clause firms negotiate away first and regret most.

## Underestimating the regulatory file

For Cameroon specifically, the baseline position is more open than much of the commentary suggests — and the parts that are open are frequently misremembered.

Foreign investors may hold **100% ownership in most activities**. For majority-foreign shareholding above 50%, a **Ministry of Trade authorisation is required**. Expatriate managers need **work and residence permits**, which is a separate track from company registration and is routinely overlooked when timelines are set.

Company registration in Cameroon can be completed quickly through a one-stop shop. The point is not that the process is slow. The point is that registration, sectoral authorisation, immigration and tax registration are four different files, taken in a particular order, and a plan that budgeted for one of them will miss by months.

On incentives: exemptions and reduced rates exist under Cameroon's investment incentive regime, but an incentive that depends on an authorisation you have not yet obtained is not a benefit to model. Price the base case and treat incentives as upside.

## Pricing the wrong risk

The financial risk that most often breaks an African entry is not political. It is currency and it is logistics.

A business earning in euros or dollars and paying costs in CFA francs carries a conversion exposure that appears in the model as a rounding error and appears in the cash flow as a crisis when the rate moves. CEMAC membership and the BEAC's fixed peg to the euro reduce some of this — and introduce their own constraints on capital movement.

Logistics is the second one. Freight from Douala to inland destinations, and onward to neighbouring markets, is not a line item to be optimised at the end of the project. Lead times vary, and a distributor's inventory promise is only as good as the corridor serving it.

Both of these belong in the entry model, with a downside case, before the first unit moves. A market that only works at today's exchange rate and an uninterrupted port is not a market you have entered.

## What a competent engagement looks like

A market entry study that is worth paying for does four things:

- **Shortlists the segment** against demand, regulation, competition and realistic pricing, and rules segments out rather than stretching one to fit.
- **Confirms the regulatory position** before any commercial commitment, including ownership limits, required authorisations and the immigration track.
- **Identifies and qualifies counterparties** — distributors, agents, joint-venture partners or acquisition targets — with a defensible basis for the shortlist.
- **Produces a roadmap with owners and dates**, and introduces counterparties while you are still deciding.

Our own engagements run four to eight weeks: one to two weeks of discovery, two to three weeks of market and entry-mode assessment, then a roadmap with partner and buyer introductions running in parallel while you execute. That is deliberately short. If the answer requires a year of study, the question is probably wrong.

## What to ask before you hire anyone

Whether you work with us or a competitor, these questions separate a serious market entry adviser from a contact database:

- **Who has actually done this in this specific market?** Not "in Africa" — in your sector, in this country, within the last five years.
- **What regulatory file will you produce, and who signs it?** A list of requirements is not a file.
- **How are counterparties qualified?** What is the shortlist criteria, and will you show the working?
- **What happens if the answer is that you should not enter?** If the answer to that question can only be yes, you are buying confirmation, not advice.
- **Do you hold conflicts?** An adviser who represents your potential partner, your competitor and your investor is working for three masters.
