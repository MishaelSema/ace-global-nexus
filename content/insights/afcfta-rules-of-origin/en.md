Most firms treat the AfCFTA as a discount they either qualify for or do not. That is the wrong mental model. Preference under the AfCFTA is an *outcome of a file* — a rules-of-origin determination, supported by a certificate — and the determination is made long before the goods leave the warehouse.

The practical consequence is simple: an exporter who has never calculated the non-originating material content of his own product cannot claim the preference, and will pay the full duty at the border without ever knowing there was a saving available.

This article sets out what the rules actually say, where the real advantage sits, and what a Cameroonian exporter has to do in practice.

## What the general rule actually says

The AfCFTA runs on a hybrid system: a general rule that applies to most tariff lines, plus product-specific rules that override it where the Council of Ministers has agreed something more demanding. The rules sit in **Appendix IV to Annex 2** of the Agreement, approved by the Council of Ministers on 12 December 2023, with a consolidated Annex 2 adopted by the AU Assembly in February 2024.

The general rule is expressed as a ceiling on imported content:

> The value of non-originating materials must not exceed **60% of the ex-works price** of the product.

Read that as the other way round: the product must show **at least 40% African value added**. A worked example from the Agreement is pasta — it must demonstrate 40% African value addition to qualify. Certain machinery lines, such as vacuum cleaners, use the same VNOM test.

There is also a **value tolerance of 15%** of the ex-works price. This allows a small shortfall against the threshold to be tolerated without losing preference entirely. It does not apply to textiles and clothing, and it does not apply to goods that are *wholly obtained* in the continent.

## Product-specific rules override the general rule

This is where exporters get caught. The general rule is a floor for capability, not a ceiling for generosity. Where a product-specific rule exists, it governs.

Two sectors are worth knowing in detail, because they are where African exporters are most active and where the rules have moved most recently:

- **Automotive.** Rules of origin for HS headings 8701 to 8716 were agreed at the Council of Ministers in Cairo in September 2025 and adopted by the AU Assembly on 14–15 February 2026. They set a **minimum 40% African content** with a VNOM ceiling of 60%, subject to review after five years.
- **Textiles and clothing.** For Chapters 61 and 62 the agreed rule is **double transformation** — manufacture from yarn, not from fabric — with exceptions for synthetic fibres and fabrics. The general chapter rule for textiles is VNOM ≤ 60%. The February 2026 AU Assembly only "took note" of a Ministerial Directive applying these textile rules, which means they are being used **provisionally** while the technical work is finalised.

One warning about widely repeated figures. You will see "20% value added" quoted as the AfCFTA textile threshold in a lot of commentary. It is not an agreed rule in any AfCFTA instrument; it traces to an illustrative assumption in a UNCTAD modelling paper. Do not build a commercial case on it.

By mid-2025 around **92.3% of tariff lines had agreed rules of origin**, with textiles, clothing and automotive the outstanding gaps — the latter two now resolved, leaving textiles the last significant file to close.

## Cumulation is where the real advantage is

The rule that gets discussed least is the one most likely to change an exporter's economics. **Annex 2, Article 8** provides three forms of cumulation:

1. **Continental cumulation (Art 8(1)).** All State Parties are treated as a single territory. African value content can be assembled anywhere on the continent.
2. **Diagonal or regional cumulation (Art 8(2)).** Originating inputs may be aggregated across State Parties for a specific bilateral or regional arrangement.
3. **Full cumulation (Art 8(3)).** Manufacturing operations performed anywhere in Africa can be aggregated, evidenced by a **supplier's or producer's declaration** in **Appendix III**.

Ordinary cumulation is evidenced by the certificate of origin or origin declaration under Article 31(1). The operative marker on the certificate itself is the **CUMULATION** endorsement in **Box 3** — if that box is empty, the treating customs authority has no basis to aggregate.

For a Cameroonian processor buying cocoa, timber, hides or minerals from across Central Africa, this is the mechanism that turns a scattered regional supply base into a single qualifying origin. Without it, the same supply chain can fail the threshold on arithmetic alone.

Note also what is **not** available: AfCFTA provides **no bilateral cumulation with third countries**. Inputs from outside the continent cannot be brought into the calculation.

## Getting the certificate of origin in Cameroon

Two bodies issue AfCFTA certificates of origin in Cameroon:

- The **Direction Générale des Douanes (DGD)**, the customs administration, at [douanes.cm](http://www.douanes.cm/douanes-cameroun/en/6930/6930/).
- The **Chamber of Commerce, Industry, Mines and Crafts (CCIMC)**, which also issues certificates digitally through the **GUCE e-GUCE** single window.

The certificate is valid for **12 months**, and an electronic certificate is permitted where national law allows.

A caution on the continental digital system: the pan-African e-certificate of origin platform **is not yet operational**. Its architecture is settled — a hybrid model where the central platform acts as a hub and issuance stays with national authorities — and the contracting tender opened on 25 June 2026 and closed on 6 August 2026, with five pilot State Parties selected from a readiness matrix. But the names of those five have not been published, and Cameroon cannot be assumed to be among them. For now, plan on a paper certificate.

The sequence in practice:

1. Confirm the destination country is an AfCFTA State Party **and** that it has gazetted a schedule of tariff commitments covering your HS code. Preference only exists where the importer's schedule actually covers your product.
2. Test the product against the general rule and any product-specific rule, and apply continental cumulation to the inputs you can source from anywhere in Africa.
3. Obtain the certificate of origin from the DGD or the CCIMC.
4. Present it to the importing country's customs, which verifies it against the notifying State Party.
5. Retain the underlying evidence — supplier's declarations for full cumulation, invoices, and production records.

Step 1 is where most effort should go, because it is cheap and it is decisive. The **e-Tariff Book** at [etariff.au-afcfta.org/mapsearch](https://etariff.au-afcfta.org/mapsearch) lets an exporter look up whether a specific destination State has actually gazetted a preferential rate for a given HS code. It is the single most useful practical tool available today, and checking it costs nothing.

## Five mistakes that disqualify a shipment

- **Assuming signature equals preference.** Preference depends on the *importer's* gazetted schedule, not on your country's membership.
- **Calculating value added without the right cost basis.** The threshold is measured against ex-works price, not FOB, not CIF.
- **Leaving Box 3 of the certificate blank.** Without the CUMULATION endorsement, regional inputs are counted separately.
- **Mixing raw and processed stages.** Double transformation rules for textiles and automotive mean an intermediate input is not automatically a qualifying input.
- **Leaving the supporting file in a drawer.** Customs verification is the importer's right. If the cost build-up cannot be reconstructed, the preference is at risk at the worst moment.

## Is it worth the effort today?

Honestly, at continental scale: not yet, for most exporters. As of January 2025, the UN Economic Commission for Africa recorded **13 certificates of origin issued across all of Central Africa** — four to Cameroon and nine to Rwanda — and Cameroon had traded only **four export consignments** under the agreement.

Cameroon has been doing it correctly where it has been used. The **first AfCFTA certificate of origin issued in Cameroon** was on 6 October 2022 to GIC AFATEX for exports to Ghana; two more followed that month to Cameroon Tea Estate and Ndawara Tea Estate, covering 38.6 kg of tea to Ghana. The **first AfCFTA import into Cameroon** arrived on 3 July 2023 — Tunisian resin from INODA, securing a 30% duty reduction. These are small consignments, but they prove the mechanism works end to end.

There is also a complication specific to Central Africa that exporters should price in: the **harmonised ECCAS–CEMAC Customs Code and the ECCAS Common External Tariff entered into force in Cameroon on 1 January 2026**. Approved on 18 October 2024 and using the HS 2022 nomenclature, it creates a second customs layer sitting over AfCFTA preferences. Which tariff applies, and in what order the rules interact, is exactly the question to put to a customs advisor before quoting a landed price.

The practical advice, then: do not restructure your business around the AfCFTA today, but **do** build the cost file, because it is cheap now and it is the thing that will be expensive to reconstruct later.
