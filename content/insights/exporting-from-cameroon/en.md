Most questions we are asked about exporting from Cameroon are really one question in disguise: *how much paperwork stands between me and a shipment that a foreign buyer will actually accept?*

The honest answer is that the process itself is orderly and not especially slow. The problems exporters encounter are almost never procedural. They are about which market you chose, which preferential regime you assumed applied, and whether the certificate you hold covers what is actually in the container.

This guide walks the chain, then deals with the part that determines whether it is worth doing.

## The single window

All foreign trade in Cameroon runs through **GUCE-GIE** — *Guichet Unique de Commerce Extérieur* — a public-private group created in 1999 and operating as the compulsory single window. It sits at 5 Boulevard Leclerc, Bonanjo, Douala.

If you are starting from outside the country, this is your first point of contact and the platform your broker or representative will operate through. There is no faster parallel route.

## The e-FORCE export chain

Customs export processing runs on the **e-FORCE Export** system through five stages, in this order:

1. **Prior approval** — the transaction is authorised before goods move.
2. **Pre-clearance** — declarations are lodged and checked.
3. **Handling** — physical examination and movement within the terminal.
4. **Clearance** — release is granted.
5. **Loading** — onto the vessel.

A third generation of the system was originally due in 2025. That deadline has moved to **2027**. Plan your documentation workflow around the current system, not the announced one.

## Pre-export formalities

The chain begins with **registration in the exporter's file at the Ministry of Trade**, followed by obtaining the **taxpayer card**. From there you obtain certificates of origin where a preferential regime applies, and file the specific export declaration.

One caveat worth stating plainly. The official Douanes procedural page could not be retrieved when this article was prepared, so the sequence above is assembled from secondary sources. Before you act on it as procedural advice, verify the current steps directly with Douanes. The order of operations is stable; specific document names and submission channels do change.

Where a preferential regime applies, the certificate is the document that matters most. A non-preferential certificate of origin is worthless for duty purposes, and a preferential certificate issued under the wrong regime will be rejected at the border. This is worth checking with the issuing authority before the goods are loaded, not after.

## Where Cameroon actually sells

This is the part most export guides get wrong, usually by asserting a regional market that does not exist yet.

Cameroon's top export destinations in **2024**:

- **Netherlands** — US$1.04bn, 19.2%
- **China** — US$900m, 16.5%
- **India** — US$550m, 10.1%
- **Italy** — US$350m, 6.4%
- **France** — US$310m, 5.7%

Compare **2023**: Netherlands US$1.15bn (23.2%), France US$615m (12.4%), India US$476m (9.6%), China US$385m (7.8%), Chad US$248m (5.0%).

Two things follow. First, Europe and Asia are the market. Your export strategy should be built around those destinations, not around a regional ambition. Second, note what is absent: **Nigeria does not appear in Cameroon's top five export destinations in either year.** Nigeria is the third-largest *import* partner, at US$554m or 21.8% of imports in 2024. If a proposal you are evaluating depends on Nigerian demand for Cameroonian goods, verify that premise before you build a model on it.

## What AfCFTA has actually delivered

The African Continental Free Trade Area is the most-discussed feature of the Cameroonian export landscape. The measured uptake is minimal.

In **2023, Africa took only 12.7% of Cameroon's exports** and supplied 9.5% of imports. The list of Cameroonian firms that have actually shipped under the preferential regime is short: **GIC Afatex** (safou fruit, dried pineapple, ginger, to Ghana), **Cameroon Tea Estate** and **Ndawara Tea Estate** (tea, to Ghana), **Inoda Industries** (resin), and **Alucam** (aluminium ingots, to Algeria).

That is the honest position, and it matters commercially in two directions. It means the preferential margin is not currently a significant revenue line — so do not model it as one. It also means the room is large: **400 products** from Central Africa already qualify, per the ECCAS Commission. Cameroon is now building the capacity to use them, with a WCO/EU/CNCC rules-of-origin workshop held in Douala from 29 June to 2 July 2026 to develop a national pool of AfCFTA rules-of-origin experts.

An exporter with a product that qualifies, working with someone who can produce the documentation correctly, has an option here that simply did not exist three years ago. It has not been priced into the market yet.

If you are assessing whether a specific product qualifies, our guide to [AfCFTA rules of origin](/insights/afcfta-rules-of-origin) sets out the continental value-added test and the cumulation rules that decide the answer.

## Getting to the water

Nearly everything moves through the **Port of Douala-Bonaberi**.

- About **12.2 million metric tonnes** of cargo in 2024, and **over 70%** of Cameroon's imports.
- Roughly **380,000 TEU** in 2024 — modest container volumes for a gateway of that size, because the port is predominantly break-bulk.
- It serves as the transit gateway for landlocked **Chad and the Central African Republic**.
- Average cargo per vessel: **11,865 tonnes**.
- Cameroon's ports in aggregate handled **23.7 million tonnes in 2025**, up 3%.

The port is undergoing a digital overhaul. The **SIP — Système d'Information Portuaire** launched on 14 November 2024 and is scheduled for completion on 14 October 2026, replacing the legacy CARGO software. It covers vessel-call management, cargo, invoicing, electronic payment of port charges, access control, truck appointment scheduling and booking. Live testing began on 3 July 2026.

**AD Ports Group** has also announced investment in a new **bulk terminal** at Douala.

Here we have to be careful. Current port handling fees, container dwell times in days and realistic clearance windows could not be verified against a primary source when this was written, and the only figures circulating came from undated documents. **We are not going to quote them.** If handling cost or clearance time is a decisive factor in your decision, get current tariffs directly from PAD and the port community, and model against what they tell you rather than against a number on a website.

## What to check before your first shipment

- **Is the preferential regime the right one, and do you hold the right certificate for it?** Not "a certificate of origin" — the correct one, for the destination, under the scheme you are claiming.
- **Does your product actually qualify?** For AfCFTA this means the rules-of-origin test, not the tariff line alone. [The rules are not intuitive](/insights/afcfta-rules-of-origin).
- **Who is your importer of record, and are they set up in the destination?** An EU buyer needs an EU entity; this is not a formality you can defer.
- **Have you costed the corridor end to end** — inland movement to the port, handling, freight, insurance, and the delay risk at each step, using verified current figures?
- **Which chamber or forwarding agent is actually on the ground?** GUCE is compulsory and effective, but the quality of the individual handling matters more than the framework.
